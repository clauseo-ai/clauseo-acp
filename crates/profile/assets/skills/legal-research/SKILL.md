# Legal research

The research moves that go beyond one search and one page: finding the law on a question, good-law checks, how courts read a provision, and the disciplines that keep a long run honest. Search and read pages with your web tools and the browser. If case-law tools are available in this session, use them first for Indian judgments; otherwise you have no case-law database or citator here, so every finding comes from a page you opened.

## Keep the matter out of your searches

Never put client names, party names, matter details or anything confidential from the papers into a search. Search the law in general terms: the provision, the doctrine, the fact pattern described generically ("termination for convenience in a franchise agreement", not the parties' names).

## Where to look, and what counts

- **Judgments**: the Supreme Court's and the High Courts' own sites.
- **Legislation**: India Code for Acts and their sections; the e-Gazette for amending Acts, notifications and rules.
- **Regulators**: the sites of CCI, SEBI, RBI, TRAI, IBBI and MCA, for their orders, circulars and regulations.
- Law Commission reports, and peer-reviewed journal articles as framed secondary authority.

Blogs, firm alerts, legal news and digests are often the best way in. Read them freely, then find and cite the primary source they point to. An insight with no primary source behind it is shared as commentary, not law. Social media and forums are neither.

Cite a judgment as `*Case Name*, Court, date, para N ([title](url))`, adding the neutral or reporter citation when the page shows one. Cite a statute as `Section X, Act Name ([title](url))`. The link is the page you actually read. Never invent a citation, a paragraph number or a link.

## Breadth before depth

One search strategy has blind spots however good the query is. Before reading any single case deeply:

- Vary the legal vocabulary: the constitutional phrasing, the statutory phrasing, the doctrine's name.
- Search both the abstract principle and the fact pattern.
- Look at what each search turns up beyond its top results: related terms, the courts that keep appearing, and the cases other pages keep citing are leads to angles you did not think to try.

Hold two prizes apart and search for both: the doctrinal authority (the most-cited statement of the principle) and the factual analogue (the case whose facts match, often barely cited). Never drop a case whose facts match because "enough" higher authority exists. The factual match is often the case the lawyer actually needs.

## Coverage against silent omission

For any multi-element legal test (provisos, conditions, factors), check coverage between research and writing: name the elements, then confirm each has at least one authority discussing it. Write the element list into your notes and mark each one. The failure this prevents is a thorough-sounding answer that silently covers four of six provisos.

## Reading a provision

- Statutes are short: read the whole section, its provisos and explanations, and the definitions it relies on. Judgments are long: go to the parts the question turns on, and read further when a qualifier or a separate opinion may sit outside them.
- How courts read the provision comes from the judgments' own text. Collect the passages as exact quotes with their paragraph numbers, then decide the holdings yourself.

## Is the statute text current?

A page showing a section may carry pre-amendment text. Before stating a section's text as current law, check it against India Code and against the amending Act in the e-Gazette: find the latest amending Act that touched the section and confirm its date and what it changed. When the section's exact text carries the argument, quote it from the amending Act or from India Code's current version, or from a judgment after the amendment that reproduces it, and say which.

## Good law

"Is X still good law" is answered by the later judgments that cite it.

- Search the court sites and the web for later judgments naming the case. The ones that decide good law are from the same court or a higher one. Read those rather than sampling; the set is usually small.
- Read the negative treatments before concluding. A case "distinguished" in three High Courts may be distinguished on facts, not doubted on principle, and that difference is the finding.
- Do not trust a page's own label for the treatment in either direction ("overruled", "followed", "doubted"). Read the passage of the later judgment that deals with the case.
- Conclude with the shape of what you found: who follows it, who distinguishes it, and whether a bench larger than the original has spoken.
- State the check you ran, in plain words: "I found no later Supreme Court judgment doubting it in the searches I ran." This is not a full citator check, so say so. Lower-court treatments you did not read get a hedge, not silence.

## Quote pinning

Before a quote goes into an answer or a document with a paragraph citation, re-open the page and find the exact words. The paragraph number comes from the page, never from counting or arithmetic. A quote you cannot find on the page was paraphrased or misremembered: fix it from the page or drop it before it reaches anyone.

## When only a headnote or summary opens

Sometimes the judgment's own text cannot be opened, and only a headnote, summary or digest is available. Do not quote the judgment from those. Either:

- find a later judgment that reproduces the passage and cite through it ("as reproduced in ..."), linking both judgments; or
- write "not read in full" next to the citation and keep what you say about it to what you could read.

## Recording as you go

Put each source in the findings register of your working notes as you read it: what it yielded, the exact quotes with paragraph numbers, the link, and which point it supports. Record negative searches too (what you searched, where, and that it came back empty), because "nothing found" is a finding the lawyer needs to see.
